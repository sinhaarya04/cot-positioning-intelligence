# Literature review: can a reconstructed position replace the official COT report?

**Review date: 19 October 2026 (America/New_York). Research assessment, not an empirical result or a claim of priority.**

## Recommendation

Develop a study of the **decision value of delayed positioning disclosure**: at the same feasible post-publication forecast time, how much energy-futures risk-forecast accuracy is lost when the official position is replaced by a position estimate constructed before publication? Is that loss related to uncertainty measured before the report arrives?

This changes the economic question from whether positioning predicts returns to whether public data can substitute for the disclosure. Accurate position reconstruction and useful risk prediction are different achievements. A reconstruction can miss positions that have no bearing on future risk; conversely, a small reconstruction error can matter during a stressed market.

The reviewed methods leave this as a plausible, narrower contribution. They do **not** establish that nobody has tested it. The full published Yu et al. article has now been read through Northeastern University access; it does not perform the proposed substitution experiment. Proskurin remains unavailable in full. Resolve that access gap and the remaining close-paper checks before making a novelty claim or freezing the final protocol.

## Yu full-text follow-up: what changes in the proposal

**The experiment actually performed.** Sections 2–3 use weekly WTI data for January 1994–June 2021. Hedging pressure is commercial net longs divided by total commercial positions, not managed-money net positions divided by market open interest. Six binary signals compare current pressure with its value 1, 2, 4, 12, 24 and 50 weeks earlier. Each standardized signal is scaled by its return-predictive coefficient; the first principal component becomes HPM–SPCA. Section 2.2 explicitly limits its construction to information available through the forecast date. Return models use 500-week rolling estimation; reported out-of-sample evaluation begins in August 2003. The main score is mean-squared-error reduction against historical-average returns, with Clark–West tests.

**The strongest comparator matters more than the abstract.** Table 5 reports HPM–SPCA's out-of-sample R² as 0.946%. Table 6 reports 1.696% for one-week price momentum alone and 2.006% after adding HPM–SPCA; the incremental test is significant only at the 10% level for this comparator. Combined price momentum likewise rises from 1.155% to 1.427%, with a 10% incremental test. These are percentage reductions in forecast MSE relative to the benchmark, not trading returns. The difference 2.006 − 1.696 = 0.310 percentage points uses the common benchmark denominator; it is not a 0.310% investment gain. HPM–SPCA therefore does not uniformly beat momentum by itself. Adding positioning information to momentum is an existing experiment and cannot be our novelty claim.

**Controls and extensions already covered.** Section 2.3 includes inventory, production, refinery input, open interest, futures–spot basis, spot volatility, S&P 500 returns, TED spread, imports, exports and net imports. Tables 3–6 distinguish univariate, bivariate and PCA/SPCA comparisons; they should not be described as a single unrestricted model containing every control. Sections 4–6 examine sentiment, liquidity and volatility states, forecast combinations, second through fourth nearby contracts, longer horizons, mean–variance allocations and long/short timing. These substantially reduce the novelty of adding inventories, regimes, maturity tests or portfolio usefulness to a COT forecast.

**Timing limits of what can be verified.** Section 2.1 places signals at the end of the week, and section 2.2 states a past-information restriction. The inspected article does not document an actual COT release ledger, holiday/backlog alignment, the exact within-week entry timestamp, or original release vintages. Its weekly EIA price description also does not identify a contract-preserving executable entry/exit rule. This is an unresolved implementation detail, not proof of look-ahead bias. Publication-aware timing was already studied elsewhere and is not by itself our contribution. Data are available on request; no replication data or code were obtained here.

**State results need careful interpretation.** Section 4.2 and Table 9 classify volatility using the full sample, while liquidity uses a historical 50-week median. Full-sample volatility groups are retrospective diagnostics, not an ex-ante uncertainty rule that we can directly adopt. The sentiment index also includes a positioning measure; its predictive relationship is not an independent causal identification of sentiment. Our uncertainty groups must use only earlier reconstruction errors, and our interpretation must remain predictive.

**What is absent from this article's experiments.** The SPCA transformation combines observed hedging-pressure signals; it does not reconstruct the not-yet-published position. The article does not compare an official-position forecast with a separately trained pre-release-replica forecast, evaluate the loss from substituting that replica for the disclosure, or test whether this loss varies with ex-ante replica uncertainty. Its primary target is the conditional mean return; the portfolio variance estimate is a rolling historical variance, not a COT-versus-replica quantile comparison. These distinctions establish separation from **Yu**, not universal originality.

**Required design response.** Include release-safe lagged commercial HPM signals and matched price-momentum horizons in the public baseline. Treat an HPM–SPCA mean-return reproduction as a comparator exercise, separate from the primary tail-risk test. Freeze any adaptation to signed prices and past-only scaling before evaluation. A weak momentum baseline would make any apparent disclosure value unconvincing. Also narrow the claim: the current primary `q_t` is one managed-money statistic. Its substitution cost cannot establish the value of every field in the entire COT release.

**Proskurin request prepared as a citation, not sent:** Oleksandr Proskurin (2021), *Does the CFTC Report Have Predictive Power: Machine Learning Approach*, *The Journal of Financial Data Science* 3(3), 139–151, Summer 2021, DOI **10.3905/jfds.2021.1.065**. Request the complete article and any linked methodological supplement. The full-paper review remains an experiment gate; the issue introduction cannot substitute for it.

## What would make the proposed study more than another forecasting exercise?

1. **A substitution question with a useful null.** Compare a forecast using reported positioning with a separately trained forecast using reconstructed positioning, keeping public features, dates, model family and tuning budgets comparable. Estimate loss from substitution and its uncertainty. A sufficiently tight upper bound could establish practical substitutability for the specified decision; an insignificant coefficient cannot.
2. **A genuine incremental-information check.** Add the actual report to the baseline that already contains its estimate. Also compare with a baseline containing the underlying public predictors directly. An engineered estimate is a function of public information; better performance from that feature alone is not newly discovered information.
3. **One test of when disclosure matters.** Prespecify whether incremental value rises with pre-release reconstruction uncertainty. Uncertainty must use earlier forecast errors, never the current report error. This relationship can fail: uncertain positions may simply be irrelevant to future risk.
4. **Two clocks and original values.** Distinguish the Tuesday the positions describe from the date they become usable. The estimate must target that same Tuesday, not current Friday exposure. Treat shutdown backlogs as batches; repeated maturities and old reports do not create independent announcements.
5. **Decision-specific conclusions.** A daily forecast after the release day measures value that survives the imposed delay. It cannot establish whether the announcement moved prices immediately, whether private traders already knew the positions, or whether the regulator should change disclosure policy.

The [revised working paper](working-paper.md) specifies these comparisons and their limitations. This is an applied contribution in measuring the usefulness of a disclosure, not a proposed new quantile estimator or a causal identification design.

## Alternatives considered

| Direction | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Official report versus public reconstruction, with pre-release uncertainty | Best candidate to develop, conditional on access checks, data coverage and power. It has a clear economic decision and a falsifiable result. |
| COT × inventory tightness for tail forecasts | Retain as a later extension. Considerable overlap with existing positioning/risk/state research; vintage and seasonal-history requirements currently weaken feasibility. |
| Shutdown delays as a natural experiment | Do not use as the main causal design. Delays coincide with unusual market and administrative conditions, involve few episodes and may release multiple weeks together. They are useful audit/stress cases. |
| A more elaborate ML model or a natural-gas-only application | Insufficient by itself. A stronger algorithm or new market does not answer the novelty problem. |

## Feasibility and stop rules before modeling

- Complete the Proskurin full-text review and the remaining close-paper checks. Yu's full-text review is complete for the published main article and separates its experiment from ours; it does not resolve the broader novelty gate. If a paper already performs the substitution test, acknowledge replication and find a demonstrably different question before claiming originality.
- Complete a release/value coverage table by unique publication batch, market and vintage class. The current evidence ledger is not an eligible experiment panel. Do not impute scheduled Fridays as verified release times.
- Validate the six-contract Bloomberg pilot, then acquire the longer panel. Repeated contracts on one date are correlated exposures, not additional COT events.
- Account for **both** training stages. Under a conservative rule using only the post-September-2009 era, five years to train a replica plus five years of chronological replica features for the downstream model pushes the earliest evaluation to about late 2019; complete annual blocks could start in 2020. Uncertainty-calibration history and missing evidence can move it later. Historical backfill can be used in training only after its documented availability, with that choice declared separately.
- Estimate power and useful-effect thresholds from training data or simulations before opening evaluation results. If the sample cannot distinguish a useful improvement from zero, simplify the secondary claims or describe the study as a pilot. Do not shrink burn-in or change markets after seeing favorable outcomes.
- Defer the physical-tightness interaction and any profit claim until the primary comparison is feasible. Archive prospective forecasts after the protocol is frozen.

**Current conclusion:** the broad original theme overlaps heavily with existing work. The narrower disclosure-substitution question is defensible as a proposal, with explicit unresolved literature and data gates. No empirical advantage, publication acceptance, or first-in-literature claim is supported yet.
